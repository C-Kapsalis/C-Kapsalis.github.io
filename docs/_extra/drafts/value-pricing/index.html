Every Price Is a Hypothesis. I Built an Open-Source System to Investigate It

What happens when competitor research, customer value, buyer preferences, demand analysis and delivery economics become parts of one pricing investigation?

A pricing decision usually begins with a number. A competitor charges €49. Another charges €79. The finance team wants a particular gross margin. Someone suggests €59.


But what supports that number?


Competitor prices tell us what other businesses charge, not necessarily what customers value. Cost models tell us what a business needs to earn, not what a buyer will pay. Surveys reveal stated preferences, which may differ from purchasing behavior. Historical transactions show what happened under previous conditions, but do not automatically explain what would happen at another price.


Each method answers a different question. Pricing decisions often combine their outputs without checking whether the evidence is comparable or whether the methods support the conclusion.


I built Value Pricing as an open-source research workflow for investigating those questions. It brings together market references, customer value, buyer research, demand analysis and delivery economics. It does not calculate a universally optimal price. It helps document the evidence behind a pricing decision, identify conflicts between methods and turn findings into hypotheses that can be tested with buyers.


Pricing is a research problem

Consider a fictional B2B software company reconsidering its pricing structure. It wants to know what comparable alternatives charge, which capabilities differentiate its offering, how much economic value the service creates, which buyer segments value those outcomes, how usage affects delivery costs and how observed transaction quantities vary with realized prices.


These questions require different evidence. A competitor comparison cannot establish willingness to pay. A value model cannot establish whether the business can deliver profitably. A simulated buyer panel cannot establish real purchasing behavior.

Define the decision before selecting methods

The first stage is discovery. Before selecting a research method, the workflow needs to understand what is sold, who buys it and what decision the business needs to make.

A usage-based software service requires different research from a professional-services retainer or a one-off digital product. The billing unit, customer segments, alternatives and distinction between buyer and user all affect the research design.

A study can be configured using a structured YAML specification:

decision:
  question: "Evaluate the current pricing structure"
  constraints:

  description: null

  axis: null
  bands: []

research:
  methods: []
  scenarios: []
  sensitivity_assumptions: {}
  validation_requirements: []

This is intentionally incomplete. It does not supply fictional market prices, customer shares or operating costs. Those inputs must come from the business or be labelled as assumptions in a synthetic exercise.

Understand the market before estimating a premium

A competitor comparison needs more than a list of prices. One vendor might charge €30 per month for a limited number of transactions. Another might charge €80 for a larger allowance, priority support and additional capabilities.


Comparing those numbers without considering scope, billing units and commercial terms can produce misleading conclusions.


Value Pricing records market references with their source, observation date, geography, commercial terms and relevant attributes. It distinguishes public prices from quote-based and undisclosed pricing.

Hedonic price analysis

When enough comparable offers are available, a hedonic model can investigate the association between product attributes and listed prices:

ln(Pᵢ) = β₀ + Σⱼ₌₁ᵏ βⱼXᵢⱼ + εᵢ

Here, Pᵢ is the observed listed price, Xᵢⱼ represents an attribute of offering i, and βⱼ estimates the association between that attribute and price.

The coefficients are not automatically causal premiums. An attribute may appear alongside other capabilities, different customer segments or different commercial terms.

Estimate customer value independently of market prices

Market references establish what alternatives charge. They do not establish what an alternative is worth to a particular buyer.

Economic Value to the Customer (EVC) compares an offering with the buyer's next-best alternative, considering the economic outcomes that differ between them.

Suppose a fictional software service reduces manual work. A basic value model might be:

V = H × C × R

Where:

  • H is the number of hours saved.

  • C is the economic cost per hour.

  • R is the proportion of theoretical savings that can actually be realised.

The realisation term matters. A system might theoretically save 100 hours, but if the customer can convert only 60% of those hours into productive capacity, treating all 100 hours as realised financial value overstates the benefit.

Value Pricing includes a configurable Monte Carlo workflow for scenarios where uncertainty can be represented meaningfully. The model examines how different assumptions affect the resulting value distribution.

The result is a modelled value estimate, not an observed willingness-to-pay figure.

Figure 4. From customer outcomes to a value distribution. The model connects the next-best alternative, operational savings, financial conversion and uncertainty assumptions to produce a distribution of modelled economic value.


Investigate buyer preferences without confusing simulation with research

Customers may value functionality, implementation support, service quality or different access levels. Their preferences do not necessarily translate into the same willingness to pay for every feature.


The workflow supports qualitative interviews, price-sensitivity research, Van Westendorp, Gabor-Granger, choice-based conjoint and exploratory simulated buyer research.


These methods answer different questions. Van Westendorp investigates perceived price acceptability. Gabor-Granger examines stated purchase likelihood across presented prices. Choice-based conjoint investigates preferences across combinations of attributes and prices.

Separate listed-price elasticity from transaction demand elasticity

The word elasticity appears in different parts of pricing research, but it does not always describe the same relationship.

Market-meter elasticity

The market analysis can investigate how listed prices vary with a competitor's usage allowance or another pricing metric. The synthetic market fixture plants an elasticity of 0.75 for usage-based pricing. The self-test checks whether the implementation recovers that relationship within a tolerance of 0.05.


This concerns the relationship between market-listed prices and plan limits. It is not customer demand elasticity.

Transaction-based demand elasticity

A separate module examines how observed transaction quantities vary with realised unit prices:

ε = (% change in quantity) / (% change in realised unit price)

Equivalently:

ε = (%ΔQ) / (%ΔP)

The repository implements a hierarchical Bayesian negative-binomial model. It accounts for repeated customer transactions, partial pooling across segments, seasonality and available controls such as promotions, channel and contract duration.

The synthetic demand fixture creates two fictional customer segments, 36 customers per segment and 12 monthly observations per customer. It plants a price elasticity of −0.80 and introduces deliberately invalid rows to test data validation.

The self-test checks whether segment-level estimates recover the planted value within a defined tolerance, whether their 90% posterior intervals contain the known value, whether convergence diagnostics pass, and whether insufficient price variation blocks estimation.

The model conditions on observed positive transactions, so it does not estimate purchase incidence. Historical prices may also be endogenous. Without a defensible identification design, the result is an observational association, not a causal estimate.

Bring delivery economics into the decision

A price can appeal to customers and still be commercially unsustainable.


The cost model investigates what it takes to deliver the offering. Depending on the business, inputs can include infrastructure, support, onboarding, labour, payment processing, channel commissions, fixed platform costs and capacity constraints.


The model calculates cost and contribution scenarios using configured inputs. Cost is an independent constraint in the research. It should not automatically determine what customers should pay. A cost-plus calculation can establish a minimum commercially viable price under stated assumptions, but it cannot establish perceived customer value.

Synthesis: keep the evidence streams separate

Imagine an investigation produces several observations: competitor listings suggest an association between certain capabilities and higher prices; the EVC model suggests that one customer outcome creates potential economic value; buyer research indicates a preference for additional support; transaction analysis suggests that observed quantity declines as realised prices increase; and delivery economics shows that additional support has a meaningful cost.


None independently determines the price.


The synthesis stage brings the findings together while retaining their separate evidence bases. It should expose disagreements rather than hide them in a weighted average.


The handover records the decision, selected methods and rationale, evidence and source dates, modelled results and uncertainty, assumptions, unresolved gaps, candidate pricing hypotheses where supported, trade-offs and a validation plan.


A model-selected candidate remains conditional on its inputs. It is not a guaranteed optimal price.


How the implementation is organised

Value Pricing is an open-source Agent Skill with supporting Python analysis tools. Its structure separates workflow instructions, method references, configurable templates and analytical scripts.

The project includes specialised perspectives such as a pricing strategist, competitive analyst, behavioural economist, buyer persona, CFO/unit-economics reviewer and devil's advocate. These perspectives structure different questions; they are not independent experts or empirical evidence.


The implementation includes data-handling safeguards. Scripts run only when invoked. Crawling is optional. External model calls require explicit confirmation of data transfer. The workflow is designed to work without sending confidential study material to a model provider.

Calibration against published research

The repository includes an optional calibration procedure based on Brand, Israeli and Ngwe's Using LLMs for Market Research (Harvard Business School Working Paper 23-062).


The procedure is designed to reproduce selected choice tasks using original study materials and compare simulated willingness-to-pay estimates with a human-research benchmark. The calibration script requires the relevant choice sets and original prompt template as inputs; these materials are not bundled with the repository.


It generates repeated prompts, processes responses and estimates willingness to pay using a multinomial logit model. It reports a bootstrap 90% confidence interval and the ratio between the simulated estimate and the supplied human benchmark.


The procedure does not automatically adjust the main study's estimates. Its existence should not be confused with a completed calibration experiment.

What the tests establish, and what they do not

The synthetic self-test checks analytical components against known values. It does not establish that competitor listings represent transaction prices, synthetic buyers behave like real customers, modelled value becomes realised benefit, historical price relationships are causal, or a recommended structure will maximise revenue.


The quality of the workflow depends on the research design and inputs. A sophisticated model cannot compensate for an incorrectly defined segment, a misleading counterfactual or a poorly measured cost driver.


What I want to improve next

I want to validate the methods against appropriately sourced real pricing studies, improve how evidence quality is represented across research streams, and develop a more complete treatment of purchase incidence.


I also want to make the handover between analytical components easier to inspect. A reader should be able to trace a pricing hypothesis back to its source evidence, assumptions, model and uncertainty.
